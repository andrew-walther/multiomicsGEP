Merged-Cohort PDAC Benchmark: Comprehensive Preprocessing & Model Comparison (18 Configurations)

Author

Andrew Walther

Published

May 27, 2026

Study Design

This report compares 18 model configurations for joint multi-cohort PDAC analysis, trained on merged TCGA_PAAD (RNA-seq, n=144) and CPTAC (proteomics, n=129) data and evaluated on five independent external cohorts. The benchmark extends an initial six-configuration comparison (M1–M6) by adding three additional preprocessing strategies: joint quantile normalization without rank transform (M7/M8, M13/M14), joint z-standardization (M9/M10, M15/M16), and log transform only (M11/M12, M17/M18).

Goal: Determine the optimal preprocessing strategy and model form for merged multi-platform training data, using cross-validated C-index (1-SE rule, biological floor K ≥ 3) for factor count selection.

Biological K floor: K_final = max(K_1se, 3). Motivation: DeSurv (Young et al. 2025) identifies K=3–4 programs; a manuscript claiming multiple gene expression programs requires at least K=3; K=3 has higher CV C-index and lower fold SE than K=2 for several configurations where 1-SE selects K=2.

YFB × joint QN+rank excluded: β→0 collapse is structural — all mitigation strategies (V0–V11) were exhausted (see DECISIONS.md 2026-05-22/25). Joint QN without rank transform is tested for YFB (M13/M14) to determine whether rank transform drives the collapse.

Conclusions

Winner: M5 — YFB × per-platform z-standardization × no cohort indicator (K=3)

M5 achieves the highest mean external C-index (0.626) across five independent PDAC cohorts. At K=3 (biological floor applied; 1-SE rule selected K=2), two of three factors are survival-active (K_eff=2), confirming that at least two distinct genomic programs carry prognostic information. This matches the prior K=2 result (mean C=0.625) essentially exactly — the floor adds a third factor without degrading external generalization, and provides a richer basis for biological interpretation.

Per-platform z-standardization is the only viable preprocessing for merged data

The extended comparison confirms that per-platform z-standardization is essential when training on merged RNA-seq and proteomics cohorts. Every preprocessing strategy that operates on the jointly stacked matrix without per-platform normalization produces either β→0 collapse or substantially inferior performance:

  • M7/M8 (LB × joint QN, no rank): mean C=0.598/0.615 — indistinguishable from M1/M2 (joint QN+rank: 0.599/0.619). K_eff=1 in both cases. Removing the rank transform from joint QN provides no benefit for LB.
  • M9/M10 (LB × joint z-std): K_eff=0, β→0 collapse. Joint z-standardization alone fails to resolve the platform offset in the LB survival likelihood.
  • M11/M12 (LB × log only): K_eff=0, β→0 collapse. Without distribution normalization, the RNA-seq vs. proteomics scale difference dominates the merged factor space entirely.
  • M13/M14 (YFB × joint QN, no rank): K_eff=0 — this is the critical diagnostic. Removing the rank transform does not rescue YFB from β→0 collapse on joint-QN data. The collapse is not driven by the rank transform; it is driven by the platform-dominated SVD directions that arise from joint quantile normalization of mixed-platform data.
  • M15/M16 (YFB × joint z-std): K_eff=0. Platform confounding persists.
  • M17/M18 (YFB × log only): K_eff=0. Same collapse.

The common mechanism: without per-platform standardization, the top singular vectors of the merged Y matrix align with the RNA-seq vs. proteomics platform difference rather than biological variation. Survival signal is orthogonal to these directions, so the CAVI update drives β→0 regardless of model form (LB or YFB) or normalization method (quantile, z-score, none).

Rank transform: marginal for LB, not the driver of YFB collapse

Comparing M1 (0.599) vs. M7 (0.598) for LB, and M2 (0.619) vs. M8 (0.615), the rank transform provides no measurable benefit. Both select K=6 and achieve K_eff=1. The M13/M14 result definitively rules out the rank transform as the cause of YFB β→0 on joint-QN data; the structural collapse persists with or without it.

Cohort indicator: helpful for LB, harmful for YFB at low K

Adding a cohort indicator to LB with joint QN (M1→M2: +0.02) improves performance substantially by absorbing the platform offset into an explicit covariate. For LB with per-platform preprocessing (M3→M4: +0.004), the gain is minimal — normalization already removes the bulk of the offset. For YFB with per-platform preprocessing (M5→M6: -0.013), the cohort indicator is harmful: at K=3, the cohort column competes with biological factors for genomic variance in ZF = YF, degrading the survival signal captured by β.

Recommended configuration for manuscript

Primary: M5 — YFB × per-platform z-standardization × no cohort indicator, K=3. Highest mean external C=0.626, two of three factors survival-active, and the most interpretable prediction path for external data: risk scores are computed exactly as η_new = (Y_new %% EF) %% β, with no approximation. This contrasts with LB, where new-subject predictions require an OLS approximation (L_new ≈ Y_new %% EF %% solve(t(EF) %*% EF)) that ignores the survival term in the L posterior.

Sensitivity check: M4 — LB × per-platform z-standardization × cohort indicator, K=3. Mean C=0.624; one fewer active factor (K_eff=1); explicitly models the RNA-seq vs. proteomics platform offset.

Not recommended: - M9–M18: 8 of 10 collapse to β=0; remaining 2 (M7/M8) match the already-inferior joint-QN LB baseline. - M1/M7 (lowest viable mean C ≈ 0.599/0.598). - M6 (cohort indicator harmful for YFB: mean C=0.613).
