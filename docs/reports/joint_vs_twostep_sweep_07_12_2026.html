Joint Supervised Factorization vs. Two-Step Baselines: A Survival-Strength Sweep

Published

July 12, 2026

Purpose

The supervised Bayesian matrix factorization model (SBMF, here in its Cox-on-YF parameterization, \(\eta = (YF)\beta\)) jointly fits a genomics factorization and a Cox survival model. The standard alternative is a two-step procedure: factorize the genomics data first, without using the survival outcome, then fit a Cox model on the resulting factor scores. This report asks a direct question: does joint estimation actually improve on the two-step approach, and — just as importantly — does it avoid inventing an advantage when there is no real survival signal to find?

We simulate data where the true prognostic effect size can be dialed from zero (survival carries no information) up to large, holding the genomics generative structure fixed within a scenario, and compare:

  • YFB (joint), fit with the production default \(\alpha = 0.5\).
  • PCA + Cox (two-step): principal components of the training genomics matrix, then a Cox model on the top components; held-out samples are scored by projecting onto the training rotation.
  • EBMF + Cox (two-step): an unsupervised empirical-Bayes matrix factorization (flashier, non-negative priors matching the joint model’s own priors), then a downstream Cox fit on the factor scores.
  • LB (joint), the alternative joint parameterization (\(\eta = L\beta\)), included as a secondary reference.
  • YFB at \(\alpha=0\), an internal control: forcing \(\alpha=0\) should reduce the joint model to a purely genomics-driven factorization, structurally identical to what it would learn regardless of the survival outcome.

Data: two synthetic cohorts (n=150 each), p=1000 genes, K=4 factors (K=8 in one scenario), all shared across cohorts (no cohort-specific structure — kept simple so the comparison isolates the survival-strength question). 10 replicate seeds per strength level, across 4 DGP scenarios (extended 2026-07-12 same-day from an initial 5-seed/1-scenario pass — see the Reproducing section and DECISIONS.md for why): default (real EBMF gene-program templates, the primary scenario used for Results 1–3 below), sparse_synthetic (synthetic sparse F, 2% active genes/factor, real templates off), low_snr (shared-factor amplitude 12→6), and high_K (K_shared 4→8). Full CAVI convergence (no iteration cap).

Result 1 — Equivalence when survival carries no signal (default scenario)

Method Mean C-index SE
YFB (joint) 0.523 0.009
LB (joint) 0.539 0.008
PCA + Cox 0.539 0.012
EBMF + Cox 0.544 0.011

All four sit within about 1-2 SE of each other and of chance (C = 0.5) — the joint model does not manufacture a spurious advantage when there is nothing to find. We report this honestly rather than rounding it down to a clean pass: with 10 seeds the gaps are tighter than the original 5-seed pass, but still not a razor-sharp confirmation of exact equivalence — consistent with it, not proof of it.

The joint model does not lead at every strength level — at 0.25 it is essentially tied with both two-step baselines (within noise), and PCA+Cox edges ahead at 0.50 (within 1 SE). From strength 1.0 onward YFB leads consistently, with the gap over both two-step baselines widening through strength 4.0. Honest summary, under this scenario’s DGP: the joint model’s advantage is not immediate or uniform, but it emerges reliably once genuine survival signal exists, and grows with it — this is the qualitative pattern the method’s value proposition requires. Whether it generalizes to other DGP structures is the subject of Result 4 below, and the answer there is more qualified than this table alone suggests.

Result 3 — Internal control: is \(\alpha=0\) really “survival off”?

A meaningful sanity check: at \(\alpha=0\), the joint model’s learned genomics factors (\(F\)) should be structurally identical to what it would learn from any other \(\alpha\), because under the YFB parameterization \(F\) never uses the survival outcome regardless of \(\alpha\) (only \(\beta\) does). Naively comparing \(F\) from two independently-converged fits is confounded, however: different \(\alpha\) values give different combined objective trajectories, so CAVI declares convergence after a different number of iterations for each — meaning a naive comparison contrasts two different snapshots of an otherwise-identical trajectory, not a true \(\alpha\) effect. Controlling for this (forcing both fits to run an identical, fixed number of iterations) gives an unambiguous answer:

max\(|F_{\alpha=0.5} - F_{\alpha=0}| = 0\) exactly, at every scenario, strength level, and seed tested (240/240 comparisons).

This result is exact and unambiguous, but it should not be read as independent empirical evidence the way Results 1–2 are: under the YFB parameterization, \(F\)’s own update never consumes \(\alpha\), \(\beta\), or the survival working-response quantities at all (code/update_F_surv_YFB.R), so \(\alpha\)-invariance is close to a structural consequence of the architecture rather than a discovery made by running the simulation. What the check does confirm empirically — and is worth verifying rather than assuming — is that this structural property actually holds in the fitted code (no bug lets \(\beta\) leak into \(F\)), and that the earlier, naive comparison’s non-zero differences really were a convergence-timing artifact and not a sign of a real, small alpha dependency the architecture argument would have missed.

Result 4 — Robustness across DGP structures: a genuine reversal, root-caused

Results 1–3 use the default scenario (real EBMF gene-program templates). Extending to 3 more DGP structures asks whether the joint-vs-2-step ordering found there is a general pattern or specific to that one structure. Two of the three extra scenarios (low_snr, high_K) confirm the same qualitative pattern as default — YFB pulls ahead of both two-step baselines as signal strengthens, by a wider margin under low_snr (0.815 vs. 0.708/0.654 at strength=4) and a comparable margin under high_K (0.903 vs. 0.885/0.837).

sparse_synthetic reverses the ordering entirely, and not marginally:

Strength YFB (joint) PCA+Cox EBMF+Cox
1.00 0.726 ± 0.022 0.817 ± 0.007 0.820 ± 0.007
2.00 0.808 ± 0.027 0.890 ± 0.005 0.894 ± 0.004
4.00 0.795 ± 0.027 0.934 ± 0.005 0.940 ± 0.004

At strength=4, YFB sits roughly 5 SEs below both two-step baselines. This is a real effect, not noise, and we root-caused it with a persisted, reproducible diagnostic (results/multi_cohort_sim/diagnose_factor_collapse.R, 5 seeds, output results/multi_cohort_sim/outputs/factor_collapse_diagnostic.csv) rather than reporting it as an open question:

  1. Refitting YFB at \(\alpha=0\) (survival term fully removed) shows the identical dead-factor count as the tuned fit in every one of 5 seeds tested (mean 1.8/4 dead, both cases) — ruling out anything related to the joint survival objective. This is a pure genomics-factorization problem.
  2. LB (the alternative joint parameterization, sharing the same update_L.R/update_F.R machinery) also collapses — on average more factors die than for YFB (mean 2.4/4 vs. 1.8/4 across the 5 seeds, worse in 3 of 5, better in the other 2) — confirming this is a shared CAVI vulnerability, not something specific to the Cox-on-YF reformulation, though the severity is seed-dependent rather than uniformly worse for LB.
  3. Switching SVD initialization to random initialization makes it uniformly worse: all 4 factors die in every one of the 5 seeds tested (vs. 1–2 of 4 for SVD init) — ruling out “bad initialization, fixable with a better starting point” as the explanation.

Diagnosis. It is not an amplitude-hierarchy effect — a_shared is applied identically to every shared factor in every scenario, including default, so there is no built-in amplitude ranking anywhere for CAVI to exploit. The real differentiator is loading structure: the real EBMF templates used in default/low_snr/high_K are dense (97% of genes carry a non-trivial loading in a representative factor, not a sparse point mass) with graded magnitudes and real cross-factor correlation (up to \(|r|\approx0.4\) between columns) — verified directly on the cached templates. sparse_synthetic, by contrast, gives each factor exactly 2% of genes active, uniform magnitude within a factor, and near-zero cross-factor overlap by construction (columns are independently sampled disjoint gene sets). This near-perfect symmetry — several factors of identical scale with no overlapping structure to break ties — appears to be exactly the condition under which our point_exponential-based joint CAVI can hit a degenerate fixed point: once a factor’s estimated loadings dip slightly below its (otherwise identical) siblings early in optimization, the shrinkage prior pushes it further toward zero every iteration, with no mechanism to recover, and the ELBO genuinely plateaus at this bad solution (hence the fast, “converged” fits — 6–14 iterations across all checks). EBMF avoids this because flashier fits factors greedily, one at a time on the residual — each factor only ever competes against noise, never against symmetric siblings for the same variance. PCA avoids it because it performs no sparsity-inducing shrinkage at all, so there is no collapse mechanism to trigger.

Why this doesn’t obviously threaten the real-data conclusions — but with a genuine caveat. Real gene-expression factors have the dense, graded, cross-correlated structure described above, which default never showed any collapse under (0/60 fits, either model). But the failure mode is not strictly confined to the most extreme synthetic scenario: in low_snr and high_K — both built on the same real EBMF templates as default — LB shows occasional complete collapse (10 of 180 fits, frac_recov=0; YFB shows none, 0 of 180). So the honest scoping claim is narrower than “only happens with unrealistic synthetic data”: YFB has not collapsed in any real-template scenario tested here, but LB has, occasionally, even with real templates, when SNR is low or K is larger. This is logged as a genuine, understood algorithmic limitation of the joint CAVI fitting procedure (shared by LB and YFB, with LB apparently more susceptible) — not resolved in this report; candidate follow-ups (multistart best-ELBO selection via code/fit_modular_multistart.R, or a greedy-init variant) are tracked in ROADMAP.md.

Limitations

  • Factor-recovery (correlation with the true gene programs) was also tracked; in the default scenario, PCA’s linear, orthogonal basis recovers the true factors noticeably less well than the EBNM/NMF-style methods (YFB, EBMF) — a structural mismatch between PCA and this DGP, not necessarily a general property of two-step methods. In sparse_synthetic, this reverses (see Result 4): YFB’s factor recovery collapses (~0.62–0.75) while EBMF’s stays near-perfect (~0.998).
  • LB (the alternative joint parameterization) is included for reference only; per DECISIONS.md 2026-07-12, LB’s own objective-normalization work is unresolved, and it shows the same factor-collapse vulnerability as YFB, on average somewhat more severely (see Result 4) — no claims are made about its competitiveness beyond the raw numbers shown.
  • 10 seeds gives tighter standard errors than the initial 5-seed pass, but wide-strength-range claims (especially the strength=0 equivalence check) would still benefit from more seeds.
  • Only 4 DGP scenarios were tested; two additional axes were scoped out of this pass to bound compute and are flagged, not silently dropped: n/p variation toward realistic PDAC scale, and a cohort-heterogeneity variant. Both remain open follow-ups.
  • This is a controlled simulation, not the real PDAC validation; Phase 1’s own real-data results (external mean C=0.627, DECISIONS.md 2026-07-12) remain the primary evidence for real-world performance. The factor-collapse failure mode (Result 4) has not been observed for YFB in any real-template scenario tested here, but has occurred occasionally for LB even with real templates (low_snr, high_K) — see the scoping note there for the precise, non-overclaimed boundary.
