16.3 Rashid Lab Meeting Update (November 19th, 2026)

This update covers the work since the last lab update. The November 5 meeting was cancelled, so it spans roughly November 2 to 15. The model factors standardized expression as Y ≈ LF′, where L holds patient scores and F holds gene loadings, and fits a Cox model on the program scores in the same fit. The supervision weight w_S is a power on the Cox partial likelihood, so w_S = 1 is the ordinary likelihood and larger values give survival more influence. The working model is K = 5 programs on 2,218 genes, fit to 273 TCGA and CPTAC training patients (139 deaths) with w_S = 1.

16.3.1 Progress

  1. Prelim proposal. The chapter on this project was finalized for the November 15 deadline. The oral exam is December 7.
  2. Every model choice redone on the training data alone. The gene screen, input scaling and rank had partly used outcomes from the validation cohorts. We reran each with five-fold cross-validation (CV) on the 273 training patients. The 2,218-gene screen and within-cohort z-scores stay. The one-SE rule picks K = 5, with mean within-cohort CV C 0.643 over five fold partitions. The five external series are now used only to score the frozen model. Their equal-series mean C is 0.630 (donor-bootstrap interval 0.587 to 0.670).
  3. Priors on L and F. A point-exponential prior, which gives a nonnegative factorization like DeSurv’s, did worse than the point-Laplace prior. Its CV C was 0.573 to 0.635 against 0.658 at K = 7, at most one of five folds converged, and in simulation it flagged two to four extra survival-active programs per fit. Point-Laplace stays.
  4. Priors on β. Shared point-normal and point-Laplace priors were 0.002 to 0.008 lower in CV C than a Normal prior, with about the same number of spurious survival-active programs. Normal stays.
  5. Two ways to link programs to survival (LB and YFB). LB puts the latent patient scores in the Cox model (η = Lβ). YFB puts the projection of each patient’s expression onto the loadings in the Cox model (η = (YF)β), the same kind of score DeSurv uses.
    • Factors. On the real data the two learn nearly the same programs. Their risk scores correlate 0.998, each matched pair of programs has the same 20 top genes, and Programs 2, 4 and 5 are survival-active in both. They are not identical in principle, since YF ≈ L(F′F) mixes programs that share genes. Here the overlap is small, and each latent score correlates 0.985 to 0.999 with its projection.
    • Why YFB is kept. First, YFB scores a new patient exactly as YnewF. LB has no fitted L for a new patient and must estimate it by fold-in. Second, YFB’s validation C is slightly higher: 0.655 against 0.652 in training CV (difference 0.003, SE 0.004) and 0.628 against 0.627 externally (interval −0.002 to 0.005). Both differences are within noise. Third, in simulation at w_S = 1, LB flagged more survival-active programs that match no planted program: 1.8, 1.5 and 1.2 per fit against 0.9, 0.1 and 0.0 for YFB in three of the four simulation settings. In the fourth, near the detection threshold, YFB flagged more (2.9 against 1.4).
  6. Supervised fit against two-step EBMF then Cox. The two are practically the same. The supervised-minus-two-step difference in C is +0.004 in training CV (SE 0.004) and −0.001 externally (−0.010 to 0.008). Their loadings correlate above 0.99999. The reason is that at w_S = 1 the survival term has almost no influence on the factorization compared with the expression likelihood. It supplies at most about 0.015% of the precision along each survival-active program’s loading direction. Resolving this is the first open item below.
  7. Simulation with low-variance prognostic programs. We planted a prognostic program carrying 0.05%, 0.1% or 0.2% of expression variance and compared the supervised and two-step fits at K = 5 under four survival-signal settings, from one close to the real data to one far stronger (Figure 16.1). With the very strong signal, the supervised fit had higher validation C in 29 of 30 datasets. At 0.05% it recovered the program in 4 of 10 datasets against none for the two-step fit, with C 0.112 higher. In the real-data-like setting the two fits give nearly the same programs (median loading correlation 0.98 to 0.999) and nearly the same C (mean gain 0.003 to 0.017), and neither recovers the 0.05% program. Supervision helps only when the survival signal is strong relative to the program’s share of variance. A first run at K = 7 with two of these settings, reported on November 6, gave the same pattern.
  8. Methylation alone. On 144 matched TCGA patients, methylation shares its two strongest axes with expression (canonical correlations 0.85 to 0.92) and adds a recurring immune-like program once about 50,000 CpGs are included.

Figure 16.1 shows the low-variance simulation in item 7. Supervision can find a weak prognostic program that the unsupervised factorization misses, and it moves the loadings away from the unsupervised ones, but only when the survival signal is strong. As the signal weakens, the gain in C shrinks and the loadings of the two fits converge. In the real-data-like setting, where a perfect model reaches C of about 0.69, the dots sit close to the diagonal and the loading correlations are 0.98 to 0.999. Our best models reach C of about 0.63 to 0.66 on the real PDAC data, so the real survival signal is likely in that range. This matches the real data: the supervised SSBMF and unsupervised EBMF factorizations give nearly the same programs (loadings correlate above 0.99999). It is why stronger supervision than w_S = 1 is the first open issue below.

How the simulation works and what a dot is.

  • What is simulated. Each dataset has 400 training and 120 validation patients and 2,000 genes. Expression is built from three true programs plus gene-level noise. Each program loads on a random 15% of genes with nonnegative weights. Programs 1 and 2 are strong and unrelated to survival. Program 3 is the prognostic program, and it is made weak by scaling it down to a small share of expression variance.

  • Share of expression variance (0.05%, 0.1% or 0.2%). The fraction of total expression variance that comes from the prognostic program. The smaller it is, the harder the program is to find from expression alone.

  • Survival. Survival times come from a Weibull model whose log hazard is the patient’s score on the prognostic program, with about 30% censoring.

  • How each dataset is fit. Both methods factorize the training expression at K = 5, the working rank, which is more programs than the three that exist, so neither is told the true number. The supervised SSBMF fit estimates the programs and the Cox coefficients together (w_S = 1). The two-step fit estimates the programs without survival (w_S = 0), then fits a Cox model to all of its program scores.

  • What a dot is. Each method’s full risk score, using all of its programs, is computed for the 120 validation patients and scored by Harrell’s C. A dot places the two-step C on the x-axis and the supervised C on the y-axis for the same dataset. There are 10 datasets per share and setting.

  • True-risk C. The C of the true simulated risk score on the validation patients. It is the best any model could reach.

  • The four survival settings. The true risk score SD measures how strongly the program drives survival: the larger it is, the more cleanly it separates high- and low-risk patients. The hazard ratio per SD is how many times higher the risk is for a patient one SD higher on the true risk score.

    Setting True risk score SD Hazard ratio per SD True-risk C
    Real-data-like 0.8 about 2.2 0.69
    Moderate 1.1 about 3 0.74
    Strong 2.5 about 12 0.86
    Very strong about 6 to 10 hundreds to tens of thousands 0.95

  • Relation to the real data. On the real PDAC data the working model reaches C of about 0.63 to 0.66. The real ceiling must be at least that high, so the real-data-like setting is the closest of the four. The real ceiling cannot be measured, so this is an approximation.

  • Recovery (corner tables). Among the fitted programs, take the one whose loadings correlate most with the true prognostic program. It counts as recovered when that correlation is at least 0.7 in absolute value, its survival coefficient is at least 1.96 SDs from zero, and its direction is correct. The corner tables also give the mean paired gain in validation C (supervised minus two-step).

  • Loading r (corner tables). Whether supervision changed the factorization. For each supervised program, take the largest absolute correlation of its loadings with any two-step program, and average over the programs of that dataset. The table gives the median over the 10 datasets. A value near 1 means the two fits found the same programs.

  • Run. 240 fits (10 datasets × 3 shares × 4 settings × 2 methods). All converged within 29 sweeps, with no errors or undefined C. Design and outputs are in the registration.

16.3.2 Open issues and where feedback would help

  1. Supervision is too weak to change the gene programs. With w_S as a power on the Cox likelihood, w_S = 1 leaves the programs the same as unsupervised ones. We tried three ways to raise it, and none has yet given enough influence with a stable fit:
    • A grid of fixed weights from 1 to 50 chosen by CV. CV chose 1.
    • A weight set so that survival supplies a target share of the precision along one program’s loadings. Only the smallest target, 1% (w_S ≈ 83), converged, and its loadings still correlated 0.999 with the w_S = 1 fit. In simulation that weight also lost the low-variance program the w_S = 1 fit had found (0 of 10 against 5 of 10). Targets of 5% or more (w_S ≈ 430 and up) did not converge within 500 sweeps.
    • A fixed Normal or Laplace prior on β to shrink the coefficients. Fits were stable with moderate coefficients at the 5% target for some prior scales. At the 25% and 50% targets, every fit either failed to converge or converged with coefficients far beyond what its prior allows (largest |β| up to 17 under a prior SD of 0.1). Our working explanation is that the score scale grows through F, so shrinking β alone is not enough.
    The next idea is to bound the scale of the scores and loadings, not only β. We would welcome feedback on whether a power on the likelihood is the right lever, or whether another form of supervision would work better.
  2. Balancing expression and methylation in the joint model. The M-modality model shares patient scores across data types and gives each data type its own loadings and loading prior. In the matched TCGA fit, methylation supplies 83% to 97% of the reconstruction precision in three shared programs. Down-weighting the methylation likelihood did not fix this. In simulation it shrank the methylation loadings to zero in nearly every program, and on TCGA the most methylation-heavy program stayed above 94% methylation at every weight. We need a way to put the two data types on the same scale without cutting both down to similar small feature counts (for example, about 2,000 genes and 2,000 CpGs). This work is paused until the supervision problem has a direction.
