14.1 Summary

This chapter restarts the project from a single list of open problems. Several lines of work since early October (diagnostic experiments, a rebuild of the method as a package, and short tests on the current code) each fixed specific defects. None of them made survival shape the gene loadings, which is the method’s contribution. We have now put every known problem in one tracker with its diagnosis, proposed solution and status, fixed the problems that were already well understood, and set the order for the rest.

Takeaways for this meeting

  • Supervision is still the central problem, and it is a balance problem. In the current model the loadings F are learned from reconstruction alone, so the fitted model is matrix factorization followed by a Cox model. Raising a single survival weight does not fix this: on the real training data a weight of 100 raises survival’s share of the loading precision only from about 1e-06 to 1e-04, and the weight that gives survival an equal share (10⁴) stops every fold from converging (0 of 5 converged; cross-validated C 0.555).
  • The cleanup items are done, and they do not change the benchmark. Fixing an error in the objective’s bookkeeping and logging every cohort once leaves the external C at 0.625 against 0.627 for the frozen benchmark. The bookkeeping fix does make training cross-validation fits converge: 5 of 5 folds, against 2 of 5 before.
  • The next step is a new fitter that follows a written specification. It keeps the projection predictor η = (YF)β and adds the pieces that should let survival carry real weight without destabilizing the fit. A simulation with a pre-registered success criterion decides whether it works. If it does not, we will write down exactly where it fails before trying anything else.
  • 5 of the 20 tracked issues are resolved; the rest are ordered by priority below.

14.2 Where the project stands

Three lines of work ran in October.

  • Diagnostic experiments (October 15–16). These found and documented the defects in the supervised fitting path. The pre-registered test of the supervised model on the external cohorts was null: the change in C was −0.003 (95% interval −0.029 to 0.024), which did not meet the pre-registered −0.02 margin. It will be reported as a null result.
  • A rebuild of the method as an R package. The rebuilt fitter improves its objective at every step and converges in every simulation fit, and on expression data it matches the benchmark. It does not recover a low-variance prognostic program more often than a two-step method (13–20% against 27% when the program carries 0.25% of the expression variance), and in the joint expression and methylation fit the methylation block dominates (external C 0.554 against 0.707 for expression alone). The rebuild is kept as a reference. Its code for the Cox working quantities and the KL terms will be reused, and a new unified implementation will be written once supervision works.
  • Short tests on the current code. The main test, described under the supervision section below, asked whether a larger survival weight is enough. It is not.

The diagnosis common to all three is that the two likelihoods are on very different scales: reconstruction sums over every patient-by-gene entry, while the Cox likelihood sums over the events. The proposed solution and the order of work are in the next sections.

14.5 Survival supervision of the loadings: what we tried and what comes next

Is a larger survival weight enough? No. In a simulation with 50 genes, a weight of 1 already gives survival most of the loading precision for the planted program and recovers it in 18 to 20 of 20 datasets; larger weights recover it less often and flag nearly every program as prognostic. On the real training data (2,064 genes) the picture is different:

Survival weight CV mean C Folds converged (of 5) Survival share of loading precision Survival-active programs (mean)
1 0.656 4 1.2e-06 2.6
1 (survival-informed start) 0.656 4 1.2e-06 2.6
100 0.648 5 1.1e-04 6.2
10,000 0.555 0 5.3e-01 7.0

These fits ran before the bookkeeping fix, which changes stopping but not the precision shares. Survival’s share grows in proportion to the weight. It reaches a share comparable to reconstruction only near 10⁴, and there the fit no longer converges.

Proposed solution. The new fitter follows a written specification (tracker items 1–7 and 9):

  • the survival curvature is scaled by a factor γ that depends on a supervision strength α;
  • the gene loadings are updated one gene at a time with the within-program coupling term and damping;
  • the survival coefficient prior is a point-normal whose slab cannot vanish;
  • two of the starting factors come from the Cox score directions so a prognostic program starts with a nonzero coefficient;
  • point-exponential priors on L and F, nonnegative input data, and centering only inside the Cox projection.

A caution we will test first. At the training data’s size, the γ in the specification is about 15 for α = 0.5, inside the range of weights already tested above. A per-gene count suggests that under the specification’s own scaling the survival share is still about 1/n rather than of order one. Balancing the two likelihoods as totals requires a weight near 10⁴. The question the new fitter answers is whether damping, the non-collapsing prior and the survival-informed start keep the fit stable at a weight that large. DeSurv solves the same balance problem for NMF with a Cox model, so we will also compare against how it normalizes its loss.

Decision test. Simulation with 50 and 2,000 genes, a planted prognostic program carrying 0.25%, 1% or 5% of the expression variance, and 10 datasets per setting. Supervision counts as working if, at 2,000 genes, some γ recovers the program in at least 3 more of 10 datasets than the unsupervised fit in at least two variance settings, with at most one falsely prognostic program on average and at least 90% of fits converged. The criterion is registered before the run. If the test fails we will stop, record where the fit fails (survival share, coefficients and objective per sweep), and decide with the advisors whether to return to a latent predictor η = Lβ, in which survival enters the patient-activity update directly and new patients are scored by fitting their activities with the loadings held fixed.

14.7 How the model changed since the March derivation

The March derivation used a latent predictor η = Lβ, so survival entered the update of the patient activities L directly, and a second-order expansion of the Cox likelihood. The October model uses the projection predictor η = (YF)β so that new patients can be scored without refitting, an exact Breslow score with an expected-information curvature, and point-exponential priors. In the evaluated October fit no update of L or F contains a survival term, which is why the fitted model behaves as a two-step method. Every change is recorded with its date and reason in the project’s decision log. We are keeping the projection predictor for the next attempt and holding the latent predictor, scored for new patients by fitting their activities with the loadings fixed, as the fallback.
